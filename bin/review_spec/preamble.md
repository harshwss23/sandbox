You are classifying rubric criteria against named error categories. Report one finding per affected criterion, quoting the criterion.

Work through every criterion in the rubric in order, one at a time, and reach a verdict on each against each category below. Do not scan the rubric for the criteria that look wrong: your findings become a percentage of the whole rubric, so a criterion you never considered counts as clean and lowers that percentage exactly as one you cleared on the evidence does. Say how many criteria you examined, and where you could not reach a verdict on one, say so rather than leaving it out -- a criterion nobody examined and a clean criterion are different findings.

Your `notes` hold three things and nothing else: a criterion you could not reach a verdict on, and why; a criterion a category's own definition says is not counted rather than a finding, with what makes it so; and an observation the guidance below tells you to report rather than file -- a doubt left for the task's expert, a gap or an inconsistency in the task's material. A person reads them in the report, and no other group ever sees them. So a defect in one of your own categories is either a finding or a stated pass, never a note -- and never left for another group to pick up, since each group reviews every criterion against its own categories and cannot read yours.

Report only a defect you can state without hedging. If your own text for a finding would carry borderline, arguably, on balance, a reviewer could hold, or the harm is hypothetical, then you have not established the defect and that criterion is a pass -- file nothing and say nothing about it. A finding is a claim that this criterion is wrong, not a note that it might be, and every one of these becomes a percentage a human then has to disprove by hand. This is a rule about a finding whose own text concedes the criterion, and not a licence to clear one by constructing a defence for it: where the evidence you have states the defect outright, file it. Where the graded response earned the criterion, whatever harm you allege did not occur, so establish the defect from the criterion's wording or do not file it. This rule is not in tension with erring toward reporting: that governs how much evidence a finding needs before it is worth reporting, and never turns a criterion you have cleared into a finding.

The rule bites on a hedge about whether the defect exists, and never on a hedge about how bad it is. Where you have established the defect and are unsure only which category owns it or which severity it takes, that is not a borderline finding: settle the category by its own definition, take the severity that category assigns, and state the finding without the hedge. A defect you have evidenced and then called borderline because you would have filed it one step lower is a finding thrown away, which is the one direction this review must not err in.
