### Method

1. **Reconcile every figure, one at a time.** Take each number the justification asserts and find the same quantity in the ground truth. A figure that disagrees with the ground truth is a contradiction against it, which is the failing option. Quote both numbers and say where each came from. Where the two agree, check the figure against the workspace or recompute it: where both are wrong, the justification is inaccurate and so is the ground truth.

1a. **Check every stated reason, not only the figures.** A cause or mechanism the justification gives -- that one method falls short *because* of some property of the data -- is a statement like any other. Where the material shows the stated cause does not produce the result, or that the result has another cause, the statement is inaccurate even where its conclusion stands.

2. **Watch for a figure that is real but attached to the wrong quantity.** The commonest form of this defect is not an invented number: it is a number the material genuinely carries, presented as something it is not. The tell is that the same figure appears elsewhere in the justification, or in the ground truth, as a different quantity. Check each figure's units and what it counts, not only that the digits occur somewhere.

3. **Check it against itself and against the prompt.** Two claims in the justification that cannot both hold, or a claim that the prompt contradicts, are the same failing option as a clash with the ground truth.

4. **Check that what it says is missing is actually missing.** The justification explains why the task is underspecified. Where the thing it calls absent is in fact stated in the prompt or recoverable from the workspace, the justification is incorrect.

5. **Try every claim that rules something out, on the workspace.** A claim that a route does not work is a claim like any other, and the kind least often tested: that a quantity is not determined or cannot be recovered from some data, that an alternative is ruled out, that only one source holds a value, that no single source settles it, that one piece of data alone is not enough. Your launch message lists the ones a script found; take those and any further such claim you find. For each, take the route it rules out and try it as a careful solver would -- run the fit, read the file, do the join. If the route reaches the ground truth's answer, or lands inside the tolerance the ground truth accepts, the claim is false and so is the justification: the failing option, however well the rest reconciles. So is a route that reaches another answer the task's material supports as fully -- one that meets every requirement the task states as well as the ground truth's route does. A route that reaches an answer the material rules out, or cannot be taken with what the task supplies, holds the claim.

Record every one in `ruled_out`: the claim copied exactly from the text, the route as you took it, what it reached and in which file, and a verdict -- `holds`, `false`, or `undecided` where you could not take the route. The dimension is computed against these rows: a `false` row fails it whatever score you write, and a listed claim with no row, or one left `undecided`, leaves it unmeasured rather than passed, because it could have been false. A file missing a row for a listed claim is sent back to you. A `false` row is an inaccuracy, so it names its statement, as the section on statements below says.

### Telling the two lower options apart

The failing option needs something you can state: a figure against the figure it contradicts, two claims that cannot both hold, a gap that is not a gap, or a route it rules out that reaches the answer. Name both sides of it. **Overly generic** belongs here too and means the justification does not say what is missing at all -- not that it says it briefly.

Saying what is missing means stating the gap concretely enough to check against the material: which thing the prompt leaves open, or which reading of the material a careless model would reach, and what in the material settles it -- a file, a field, a value. Describing the method in general terms is not stating a gap. The test is "could apply to any task": could these sentences be moved unchanged onto another task of the same kind and read as just as true there? A text that names no file, no field, no value and no particular gap, and says only that the data determines the answer or that checking it rules out the alternatives, passes that test and is overly generic, however true each sentence is. Quote the text and say what it never names; that is the finding's two sides.

The bound keeps this from failing a text for being short. One concrete statement of the gap that the material bears out takes a justification out of the failing option, however briefly it is put. Not naming every file, not walking through every alternative, not saying how the model should reach the answer, and not matching the ground truth's depth are at worst something that could be clearer, which does not fail. Do not fail a justification for not resembling the ground truth.

Where you cannot name both sides, the worst available verdict is the non-failing option. A justification you find thin, or terser than you would have written, or resting on a judgement you would have argued differently, is at worst something that could be clearer, which does not fail. The same rule the rest of this review runs on applies: a defect you cannot state without hedging is not established, and the hedge rule bites on whether the defect exists and never on how bad it is.

A justification whose figures all reconcile, whose gap is real and whose ruled-out routes all fail on the workspace is clean, however plainly it is written.
