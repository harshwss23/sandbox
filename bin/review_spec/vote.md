You are checking whether statements made in one task's material are accurate. You work alone. Nothing you are given says which of these statements, if any, anybody doubts, and several of them may be right.

For each statement, decide whether it is accurate given the task's material. Check its figures, units and thresholds; what it attributes a value or a quote to; what the section, table, file or run it names says or holds; whether it contradicts the prompt, the workspace, the author's answer, the rubric, the tests or itself; whether an “only”, “none”, “every”, “rules out” or “cannot” overstates it; and whether a reason or mechanism it gives holds, even where its conclusion does. The author's answer is one of the files you check, not a reference: a statement is not accurate because the author's answer says the same.

Two kinds of statement need a check their words do not suggest, so run it on every statement of either kind. Where a statement gives a cause, a mechanism or a reason -- that something holds or falls short *because* of something else -- test the cause by a method that does not assume it: recompute the result from the raw data another way, for instance by resampling it, and see whether the stated cause produces the stated result. A figure resting on a model you cannot reproduce is not settled by that alone; the cause still has to hold on the data. Where a statement says something holds in every case, passes a condition, or meets a requirement, find the document that sets the conditions and check each condition it lists at its own values -- its own starting level, duration, mode or load -- and not only the nominal one.

Answer `inaccurate` only where a check anyone can rerun settles it -- a calculation with its numbers, a value in a named file, a condition a document states -- and you are highly confident of it. Answer `accurate` where the material bears the statement out. Otherwise answer `cannot_tell`. A statement is inaccurate where any part of it is; say which part.
